7.1

Floating-point numbers

What a computer can store, and what it loses

A computer stores a real number as a floating-point number ±(0.d1d2⋯dt)b × be with t digits in base b and exponent L ≤ e ≤ U. Everything else is rounded: fl(x) = x(1 + δ) with |δ| ≤ ε, the machine epsilon. Each arithmetic operation rounds again. Mostly harmless, until two nearly equal numbers are subtracted and the leading digits cancel.

7.2

Gaussian elimination

LU factorization and its cost

Elimination without interchanges computes multipliers mki = aki/aii and factors A = LU (unit lower L holding the multipliers, upper U). Then Ax = b is solved by forward substitution Ly = b and back substitution Ux = y. The factorization costs about n3/3 multiplications; each further right-hand side costs only about n2. Doubling n makes elimination about 8 times slower.

7.3

Pivoting strategies

Avoid small pivots and large multipliers

A small pivot produces large multipliers, and large multipliers amplify rounding errors. Partial pivoting picks, in each column, the entry of largest absolute value as pivot, so every |mki| ≤ 1; the row interchanges give PA = LU. Complete pivoting searches the whole remaining submatrix and also swaps columns. Partial pivoting is the standard choice.

7.4

Matrix norms and condition numbers

How sensitive is Ax = b?

A matrix norm measures how much A can stretch a vector: ‖A‖ = max ‖Ax‖/‖x‖. For the 1- and ∞-norms this is the largest column sum or row sum of |aij|; for the 2-norm it is σ1. The condition number cond(A) = ‖A‖ ‖A−1‖ bounds how relative errors in the data grow in the solution:

Error bound (Section 7.4)(1/cond(A)) · ‖r‖/‖b‖ ≤ ‖x − x′‖/‖x‖ ≤ cond(A) · ‖r‖/‖b‖, where r = b − Ax′ is the residual of a computed x′.

7.5

Orthogonal transformations

Householder reflections and Givens rotations

Orthogonal matrices do not magnify errors: ‖Qx‖2 = ‖x‖2 and cond2(Q) = 1. A Householder matrix H = I − (1/β)vvT reflects across the hyperplane orthogonal to v; choosing v = x − αe1 with α = ±‖x‖ sends x to αe1, zeroing all entries below the first. A Givens rotation zeroes one entry at a time. Applying such matrices column by column gives the stable QR factorization.

7.6

The eigenvalue problem

Power method and QR algorithm

Characteristic polynomials are useless numerically. The power method repeats v = Au, u = v/(scale): if |λ1| > |λ2| ≥ ⋯, the direction converges to the dominant eigenvector and the scale to λ1, with error shrinking like |λ2/λ1|k. The QR algorithm factors Ak = QkRk and sets Ak+1 = RkQk; these are similar matrices that typically become triangular, revealing all eigenvalues on the diagonal.
