6.1

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Directions a matrix only stretches

A nonzero x with Ax = λx is an eigenvector and λ its eigenvalue. Rewriting as (A − λI)x = 0 shows that λ is an eigenvalue exactly when A − λI is singular, i.e. det(A − λI) = 0. That characteristic polynomial has degree n; its roots (possibly complex, possibly repeated) are the eigenvalues, with sum tr A and product det A. The eigenvectors for λ, together with 0, form the eigenspace N(A − λI).

6.2

Systems of linear differential equations

Y′ = AY

If Ax = λx, then Y(t) = eλtx solves Y′ = AY. With n independent eigenvectors every solution is Y = c1eλ1tx1 + ⋯ + cneλntxn, and the initial value fixes c = X−1Y0. The signs of the eigenvalues decide the long-run behaviour; complex eigenvalues λ = a ± bi make solutions rotate.

6.3

Diagonalization

A = XDX⁻¹, powers, Markov chains, PageRank

Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are independent (Theorem 6.3.1). If A has n independent eigenvectors, put them in the columns of X: then X−1AX = D is diagonal and Ak = XDkX−1. A matrix with fewer independent eigenvectors is defective.

A Markov chain with transition matrix A (nonnegative columns summing to 1) evolves by xk+1 = Axk. Since λ = 1 is an eigenvalue and the others usually have |λ| < 1, the chain settles at the eigenvector for 1, the steady-state vector. Google's PageRank is this vector for the web's link matrix.

6.4

Hermitian matrices

Real eigenvalues, orthonormal eigenvectors

For complex vectors the inner product is ⟨z, w⟩ = wHz, where H means conjugate transpose. A matrix with MH = M is Hermitian (for real matrices: symmetric). Its eigenvalues are real and eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal (Theorem 6.4.1). The Spectral Theorem goes further: a real symmetric A can be written A = QDQT with Q orthogonal. Every square matrix is at least unitarily similar to an upper triangular one (Schur).

6.5

The singular value decomposition

A = UΣVᵀ, low-rank approximation

Every m × n matrix factors as A = UΣVT with U, V orthogonal and Σ diagonal with singular values σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ ⋯ ≥ 0. The σi2 are the eigenvalues of ATA, the columns of V its eigenvectors, and Avi = σiui. The rank is the number of nonzero σ's. Keeping the k largest terms of A = ΣσiuiviT gives the closest matrix of rank k (Theorem 6.5.3), the basis of image compression.

6.6

Quadratic forms

Principal axes, definiteness

A quadratic form is f(x) = xTAx with A symmetric; the cross term 2bxy puts b in both off-diagonal places. Rotating to the orthonormal eigenvectors of A removes the cross terms: f = λ1y12 + ⋯ + λnyn2 (Principal Axes Theorem). So the signs of the eigenvalues decide everything: all positive means positive definite (level curves are ellipses, a minimum), mixed signs mean indefinite (hyperbolas, a saddle).

6.7

Positive definite matrices

Five equivalent tests; Cholesky

For a symmetric A the following are equivalent (Theorem 6.7.1): A is positive definite; all leading principal submatrices have positive determinants; Gaussian elimination without row interchanges gives positive pivots; A = LDLT with L unit lower triangular and positive diagonal D; and A = LLT with L lower triangular with positive diagonal (the Cholesky factorization). 6.8

Nonnegative matrices

Perron–Frobenius, economic models

A matrix with all entries positive has a positive real eigenvalue r that is strictly larger in absolute value than every other eigenvalue, with an eigenvector of positive entries (Perron's Theorem). For irreducible nonnegative matrices the same holds with weak inequalities (Frobenius). This is why Markov chains with positive transition matrices converge, and why Leontief's open model x = Ax + d has a nonnegative solution x = (I − A)−1d when the column sums of A are less than 1.
